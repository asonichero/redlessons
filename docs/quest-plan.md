# Little Red's Lessons — Quest Lines by Phase

22 October 2026. Build plan: see [build-plan.md](build-plan.md).

## Overview

Wolfe is the main story, and every other quest line feeds it. The game runs in three acts of three phases each. Most quest lines open early and finish by the end of Act 1 or Act 2, then come back in Act 3 as help in the search for Hulda.

- **Act 1, Little Red:** the village. Red takes up the cloak, builds trust with the village figures and finishes their quests; the spring fair. The wood only on Grandmother's herb paths and the way to her cabin, where Holt is met late in the act.
- **Act 2, Further In:** the wood, explored. Wolfe met, the Barrows and the Shoe, Wolfe drawing her in, Hulda going quiet unnoticed, and the errand with cake and wine at the end.
- **Act 3, Rose:** the search for Hulda, the trap, and Wolfe brought to justice, faced as Rose.

The principle running through all three acts: every authority over Red is one she chose, and her name, Rose, stops any of them at once. Everyone in the game is an adult; only Red acts otherwise, by her own choice. Between others the same holds: authority is given by the one who submits to it (the smith's over Tom is a master's, not a father's), never by a third party's blessing. Wolfe is the only figure who earns trust in private rather than in front of others, and that difference is what the player learns to read.

### Setting and voices

The village is somewhere in the north of England, in a time when few people travel far. Villagers speak the local Northern English, most broadly the old ones. **Old Halloran** is the place to enjoy it: *aye*, *nay*, *nowt* and *owt*, *summat*, *lass*, *happen* ("perhaps"), *mind*, *reckon*, the odd *thee* and *tha*, *gradely* ("fine, proper"), *mardy* ("sulky"). He's still always formal with her: "Miss Rose". **The Barrows** aren't local, and it shows the moment they speak. They're Scots from not far over the Border: *aye*, *ken*, *wee*, *bairn*, *dinnae*, *cannae*, *hame*, *hen*. That puts the two dialects side by side, near each other but plainly not the same.

Rose and her mother are **the Ridleys** (her late father's name). To the village Rose is Miss Rose, and Miss Ridley when someone means business, as the boys are "young Master Ferrier" and "young Master Dunn"; Maren is Mistress Ridley. Little Red is only for those she's let in.

## Systems the quest lines rely on

Four systems gate and colour the quests: reputation, openness, time, and the safe word.

| System | What it tracks | What it drives |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Wilful | Defying a direct instruction: off the path, past curfew, refusing a task | Holt's and Maren's responses; Willie Winkie's catches |
| Mischievous | Playful trouble that harms no one much: pranks, dawdling, teasing | Tom's dares; Mistress Abney's tolerance |
| Naughty | Wrongdoing that costs someone else: theft, lies, broken promises | Mistress Pennock (the pie); Mistress Abney (promises) |
| Openness, per person | Whom Red has told what being Little Red means | Unlocks that figure taking her in hand |
| Openness, village-wide | How many people know | Willie Winkie's standing; how freely villagers correct her |
| Wolfe's disclosure (hidden) | What Red has told Wolfe | What he can use: the forged note, the cabin scene, how convincing he is |
| Day and night | Curfew, chores, visits | Willie Winkie after dark; dusk as a risk in the wood |
| Calendar | Days pass as quests need them; some quests share a day, some take a whole day | Hulda's silence lasting weeks without anyone noticing |

Tom makes mischievous and wilful options come up more often; Peter makes steadier ones come up. Neither path is the right one.

Rose's name works the same with everyone who might correct her, Willie Winkie and Holt included. Each answers it in character, and each stops at once. The Shoe is the inversion: there she's Rose, the house uses traffic lights, and her word is Red, which is also the house's word for stop.

## Days and errands

From 1.2 the days have a pattern, agreed with Maren in the chores conversation.

- **Mama's garden**, the one chore that's hers every day. (Mary, quite contrary: it's Maren's garden, and Red tends it.) Silver bells, cockle shells and pretty maids all in a row, between the beds that feed them (onions, cabbages, beans). Red waters the flowers and only waters them; she pulls the weeds and tends the vegetables, working from the paths between the rows, which she has to walk carefully. The work varies day to day (a few weeds, the dry rows, a job or two; no watering after rain), and some days are her mother's: when she has the time, and the day after a correction for the garden. A step into the flowers tramples them out of their row, and their petals cling to her boots for a few hours; anyone who sees them fall in the village may put two and two together.
- **Her mother looks round** every few days, at dusk. If her flowers have been trodden, she asks Red about it. Owning up: a correction for the carelessness. A lie: believed, unless it was that day and the petals are still on her boots (then a heavier correction, for both). But if anyone in the village saw (or, sometimes, someone passing in the lane), her mother hears of it the next day, and that evening the correction is for the carelessness and the lie together.
- **An errand on most days.** Over breakfast Maren mentions something that's been asked for, and asks Red whether she'll do it. Some days there's none, and the day is Red's own.
- **The rest of the day** is hers: the work at the bakery and the inn, Hulda's herbs, Halloran's farm, Tom's dares, and home by the curfew bell.
- **Evenings.** Home after six of her own accord, her mother has a word: whatever she's learned that calls for a correction (in after the bell; brought home by the Watchman; the pie; the garden), one correction an evening, and the rest waits. Anything someone else has already corrected her for is a reprieve, whether her mother heard it from them or Red tells her. Nothing owed, she's asked to help with supper (yes, an honest no, or a "won't"). Then supper and bed.
- **Tom gets in the way.** On some of his free days (after smithing hours, or a day off), Tom finds her: a story or a dare, while the clock runs, keeping her from her errand or the garden if she has one still to do. She can get away early, or stay; the dares (the curfew bell rung at midday, a knock at the Dunns' door with the Watchman asleep) are mischievous, and count toward his Dares step.

**An errand is a request, not an order.** Red can say yes or no, and how she says no is coloured by her temperament (her stats and what's happened lately):

- **An honest refusal** that's about feeling too Little for it that day, however she puts it ("Not today, Mama. I can't be anybody's today."), carries no penalty. Maren takes it as it's meant.
- **An obstinate refusal** (sulking, flat defiance, a flounce) is wilful. Three in a week, and Maren gives her a **maintenance correction**: not for any one thing, but to set her right. It reminds the player of the loop (the errands, the promises, the bell) and settles her wilfulness.

**Once she's said yes, it's a promise.** Each errand has a time by which it must be done, or at least begun: a window, shown in the journal, so the day has some jeopardy. Begun in time, it's kept, however long it takes. Let slide, it's a broken promise: naughty, and the kind of thing Mistress Abney minds.

**Scenes don't use the clock.** Once she's begun an errand's scene (the shift at the bakery, sitting with Bess), it plays out without the day passing under it; when she's done, the clock moves on by the time the work would have taken. Walking between places, idling and dawdling are what spend the day.

## Quest lines by character

Each step is tagged with its phase: 1.1 The Cloak, 1.2 The Village, 1.3 Into the Wood, 2.1 Further In, 2.2 The Shoe, 2.3 The Errand, 3.1 The Reckoning, 3.2 The Search, 3.3 Justice.

### Wolfe (main story)

What he wants: Hulda's craft, for his own gain rather than for what she uses it for, and Red, plainly and carnally.

1. **2.1** First meeting, at the stream, when Red is alone: Act 2 opens on it. Charming and interested in her.
2. **2.1** Chance meetings, only ever when she's alone. He asks far more than he tells. Every answer she gives him is tracked.
3. **2.2** He learns about Hulda, the cabin and Little Red, and suggests keeping things between them. Off screen, he goes after Hulda for her craft. She escapes, injured, and hides deeper in the wood; he intercepts her messages.
4. **2.3** He forges the note asking for Red with cake and wine. He's waiting at the cabin, saying Hulda will be back soon.
5. **2.3** The noticing: the cold teapot, the shawl on the wrong chair, him calling her Little Red. She can't get away on her own; she's at his mercy until Holt intervenes. What she noticed is what she can tell Holt afterwards, and it becomes leads in 3.2.
6. **3.2** Hunted. Leads from each relationship close in on him.
7. **3.3** Trapped by Red and Hulda, using what Red knows of him. Brought before the village by Rose.

### Maren (ongoing)

1. **1.1** The homecoming, the safe word, the first correction.
2. **1.2** Agreed chores that aren't too grown: Mary's garden, small errands, curfew.
3. **1.2** The fair dress (below).
4. **1.3** Decides whether to give Willie Winkie her blessing, after his first catch.
5. **2.2** Hands Red the mending for the Shoe. Then Mistress Abney's daughter's confinement keeps Maren and Red busy in the village for weeks, so Hulda's silence passes unseen. Scripted, not a player choice.
6. **2.3** Receives the forged note and sends Red without thinking through the danger.
7. **3.1** When Holt brings Red home, Maren pours out apologies to them both. Holt says she shares the blame; she agrees and asks him to hold her to account. Red, and the player, witness it.
8. **3.3** Stands with Rose at the end.

**Her day (Act 1).** It's planting time: most days Maren is out at the Hallorans' farm from after breakfast (7.30), home at 12.30 for dinner with Red, and out again till supper (6). Some days she has needlework, and works at home for a part of the day instead. Dinner is Red's midday check-in: the meal fills her capacity again, and Maren looks over her morning (the garden, the errand, anything she's seen), never correcting there, but letting her know what's coming if she doesn't put it right.

**The fair dress** (1.2; the rhyme behind it, *Little Polly Flinders*, never named: the player makes the connection if they know it). Over her needlework days Maren makes Red a new dress for the spring fair: the first new thing Rose has had in years, made with her mother's time. Bess has her own story of a new frock spoilt by the hearth as a girl, and of her mother catching her; told as Bess's, not as a warning.
  - **When.** On the first Sixth Day after the fair's gates are met, the planting at the Hallorans' is finished. The fair is the Sixth Day after that, a week on: its Lay day is the recovery, and the stalls may stand till First Day to come down. That Sixth Day is no baking day: everyone's at the fair.
  - **The week before: the dress.** With the planting done, Maren is home more, and the dress takes four sessions of her needlework through the week. The last has a fitting: Red's expected home at a set time to try it on, and Maren makes the last adjustments so it's ready on the day (late, and the fitting waits on her, as dinner would). Bess's story of her spoilt frock comes after that session if Red talks to her, or is held to the fair (told to Red when she brings Bess her fairing).
  - **The week before, in the village.** Everyone who works is busier, getting ready: the Pennocks baking ahead for the fair, Mistress Abney brewing and airing the rooms for visitors, Master Ferrier with every hinge and hook in the village wanting seeing to before the stalls go up, the Thatchers with hurdles and the green to clear. More of them ask Red to help, on the day, as Pennock and Abney offer their afternoons now: more chances for trust before the fair, and more pulls on her capacity, alongside the dress and her mother's own errands.
  - **The day's rules.** No garden, no errand, no shifts: only the fair's errands, Maren's and Hulda's (not optional), and the other traders' (any of which she may turn down). Maren at her stall all day. The fair goes on till ten, and Red's curfew is lifted for it.
  - **Who's there.** Every villager, at the fair rather than their usual places. Hulda in the village for the day, at her stall beside Maren's; no Holt yet. Visitors, without filler bodies: the carrier's men (Ned, Amos, Kit), Master and Mistress Thatcher (Agnes's parents), the Miller, Old Halloran, and Bo's parents. (No Mistress Dunn: Master William is a widower, which is why Maren sends him pies.)
  - **The dancing.** A fiddler (the Miller, proposed: Georgie's master, playing as his apprentice spoils it). Red dances with Peter, awkward or easy as his trust is; the moment broken by Georgie's kiss (Agnes and Georgie, step 2).
  - **Fair day, on the green.** Maren has a stall: she sells flowers from Mary's garden; Hulda sells her medicines beside her. Their errands are not optional: Maren's (more flowers cut and brought from the garden, change, her dinner), Hulda's (more of the herbs Red has learnt, fetched from where they grow). Other sellers ask favours too, any of which Red may turn down to spare her capacity: Master Ferrier wants something brought from the forge (mind the soot underfoot); others need no risky path at all. Bo can't leave her flock to chase a sheep that's got out of the pen into a crop row: there's one clean way to it, and dirt by any other. A fairing brought back to Bess, who can't go. At the dancing, Georgie kisses Agnes and runs (Agnes and Georgie, step 2).
  - **The garden that day.** The lettuce and onion beds are one, and the cockle shells and silver bells one, each reached only from one side. With flowers cut for the stall there are gaps in the beds, where she can step in to reach across without harm, if she's careful: hurrying through the beds is the quick way, and spoils the dress and the flowers.
  - **The dress shows it**: earth, soot, a wet or torn hem. Every shortcut is hers to take; nothing is refused.
  - **After.** Clean: Maren's quiet pride; Bess wants every detail. A little spoiled, owned at supper: washed and mended with Maren the next morning (a needlework morning, restoring). Spoiled, or hidden: an evening correction, last thing, for spoiling her new clothes (and for the hiding, if she hid it), then the mending. No one else corrects her for it: it's Maren's dress.
  - **Gated** on more than the day: before Bess's and Mistress Abney's lines are resolved; after the inn shift's been worked, the first of Hulda's herbs learnt, and Bo met.

### Hulda

1. **1.1** The cloak, and calling her Little Red.
2. **1.2** Regular visits. Herb errands that teach Red a little of her craft.
3. **1.3** Introduces Holt on a visit.
4. **2.1** Last visit before she goes quiet. Uneasy; mentions a stranger she doesn't like the look of.
5. **2.2 to 3.1** Paused: missing. Hiding, injured, deeper in the wood.
6. **3.2** Her signs, read by Red using what she learned. Found.
7. **3.3** Lays the trap with Red.

### Peter (across all acts)

**Peter Fowler** lives with his grandfather, **Master Fowler**, on their smallholding where the meadow meets the wood, between Red's cottage and the beech. Peter calls him Grandad. They keep fowl (a duck among them), a cat, and a garden. Peter's trade is the family's: he's a fowler, netting and snaring birds and calling them with a little pipe, so Tom calls him **Piper** (and won't stop reciting "Peter Piper picked a peck…" at him). Master Fowler is an old friend of Hulda's, and grumbles about the wood and what might come out of it. Peter is steady enough that his grandfather hasn't had to take him in hand in years. The house is where the player finds Peter between quests, at the nets, the coop or the gate.

1. **1.1** The R and P on the beech. Steady, and quiet around her.
2. **1.2** Their friendship as it is now; meetings at the beech.
3. **1.3** Notices she's changed. Asks, and she decides how much to tell him.
4. **2.1** Paused until she's met the Barrows.
5. **2.2** Their first try at holding each other to account as equals, modelled on what she saw at the Barrows'.
6. **2.3** Optional, and only after step 5: later, the two of them exploring it as **Peter and Rose**, not Little Red. Peter's deftness with ropes and knots, the fowler's trade, becomes part of how they experiment. It's careful and agreed between them, and true to him: steady hands, knots that come loose with one pull, a knife always within reach, and her word stopping it at once. They may want a word of their own for it; *Beech*, for the R and P on the tree, would fit. At the Shoe, by contrast, her word is the house's own, Red.
7. **2.3** Uneasy about the stranger in the wood, and says so.
8. **3.2** The first to say something is wrong with Hulda's silence: his grandfather has noticed she's stopped coming by.
9. **3.3** Stands with her, and helps lay the trap: he knows snares, nets and rope. If step 6 happened, Rose knows his knots too, and ties one herself. It's the same rope as before, this time used against the one figure who never asked. Epilogue: their agreement, in their own words.

### Tom and the smith

1. **1.1** Tom, her partner in mischief. Impulsive where Peter is steady.
2. **1.2** Dares. She sees or hears how his father, as master and father, takes him in hand. His father has also given other villagers authority over him.
3. **1.2** The pie: once both Mistress Pennock and Tom trust her enough that she could be open with either, Tom dares her to take it. Mistress Pennock catches them and marches them both to the smith, who gives her leave to deal with Tom herself; she sends Red home to her mother. Red obeys (then tells her mother, or keeps it back and is found out the next evening, when her mother and Mistress Pennock have talked), or tells Mistress Pennock what being Little Red means and asks for the same as Tom. Obeying, the pie comes round again some days on ("it went fine last time", Tom says), the choice with it. Telling, Mistress Pennock disciplines them together in the bakery. Tom has his own safe word and doesn't use it.
4. **1.2** She tells Tom her truth (optional, gated on trust).
5. **1.3** Folded in: the smith takes her in hand by the same standard as Tom, sometimes together after shared mischief. Quest complete.
6. **3.2** Called back: the smith opens a lock or chain; Tom's recklessness is finally useful.

### Mistress Pennock (the bakery)

1. **1.1** Asks after Red's prospects.
2. **1.2** Red helps at the bakery.
3. **1.2** Tom dares Red to take a pie, and they're caught. This is where Red first tells Mistress Pennock her truth, asking to share Tom's correction. Mistress Pennock takes them both in hand together: Tom by his father's leave, Red by her own.
4. **1.3** Trust deepens. Quest complete.
5. **2.3** Bakes the cake for the errand.
6. **3.2** Called back: who ordered the cake, and who brought the note.

### Mistress Abney (the inn)

1. **1.1** Asks Red to sit up with her daughter. Best played as Rose.
2. **1.2** Work at the inn. A promise Red is tempted to break: to come at six and sit with Bess while Mistress Abney's at market. That evening Tom, Agnes or Peter comes to find her with something better; kept, an evening with Bess and Mistress Abney walking her home; broken, Bess alone, Mistress Abney cold until Red says sorry, and her mother told.
3. **1.3** Trust, disclosure, and taken in hand. Quest complete.
4. **2.2** Her daughter's confinement. Maren attends, and Red helps at the inn and at home for weeks, as Rose. This is what keeps Red out of the wood.
5. **2.3** Supplies the wine.
6. **3.2** Called back: travellers' gossip; Wolfe may have lodged there.

### Bess Abney (her daughter)

Elizabeth (Bess) Shafto by marriage, still Bess Abney to the village. Twenty-six, Mistress Abney's youngest; her older brothers and sisters have long since moved away. Slight all her life, and a little sickly as a child, which is half of why her mother hovers. She married Robert Shafto (a bonny Bobby Shafto: the carrier's man, based in the market town, who met her at the Wheatsheaf on carrier nights and called her Curly Locks) and moved away with him; he died on the road last winter, eight months ago, and she came home to the Wheatsheaf. The carrier's men she serves knew him. She sings *Bye, Baby Bunting* to the baby. She found she was with child soon after he died, and she sees the baby as a blessing: her mother's fear that grief will harm her, or the baby, is mostly misplaced. Near her time now, she's kept sitting all day (on the bench by the inn door, or in her chair by the hearth).

Fearless, wry, and a watcher ("You learn a great deal, sitting"). Practically, she's a little worried about the birth, but she wouldn't say so. She doesn't talk much about her husband. She's eight years older than Rose and remembers her as a child. She guesses, before anyone tells her, what Rose is doing in being Little Red, and treats her subtly differently once she has, without letting on that's why, until Red tells her the truth.

1. **1.2** On her bench and at the inn: company, if Red sits with her (only sitting brings her trust). The inn's shifts are hers to run from her chair while her mother's out; in their lulls, her story comes out a piece at a time. Abney's promise: the evening by the fire with her.
2. **2.2** Her confinement (see Mistress Abney).

### Agnes and Georgie (Georgie Porgie)

**Agnes Thatcher** is at the well most days, and knows everything said at it. **Georgie Dunn**, the miller's apprentice and the Watchman's son, has been sweet on her for a year, and wrote her a poem. Both grown, both Red's friends since they were small. The rhyme: Georgie Porgie kissed the girls and made them cry; when the boys came out to play, Georgie Porgie ran away. Never named in the game.

1. **1.1–1.2** Village colour: the poem, Georgie mooning at the well, Agnes pretending not to notice.
2. **1.2 The fair.** Carried away by the day, Georgie kisses Agnes in front of everyone, at the dancing: she hadn't asked for it, and it makes her small in front of the whole green. She cries. Tom and the lads come over roaring at it, and Georgie runs, leaving her standing there. Red sees it, as one of the fair's encounters; nothing she does on the day mends it.
3. **1.3** Agnes stops coming to the well. Red finds her elsewhere. She doesn't want an apology: she wants him to feel it as she did, and doesn't know how that could be.
4. **1.3** With Agnes's trust high enough, Red tells her what being Little Red means, and what makes a correction work: asked for, a word that stops it, and the after mattering as much. Agnes becomes the friend Red confides in (about Peter, later).
5. **1.3** Georgie, his trust high (after Lay night, when he walked her home and it was awkward and honest), is ashamed, and running made it worse. Red puts it to him plainly; it's his to choose, and both answers go on:
   - **Yes:** he asks Agnes to hold him to account. Red sets the terms with both of them: where, his word to stop it, and whether she's there (Agnes asks her to be). It's done in the wood, off the herb path, away from the village's eyes and ears: Agnes and Georgie, Red watching. Then the after, the two of them; and the well is the well again.
   - **No:** he says sorry, and means it, and it isn't enough. Agnes is civil to him; the well is quieter than it was.
   No blessing is asked of anyone: it's between the two of them, and his yes is all the authority it needs.

### The Barrows

**Backstory.** Mother and Father Barrow have been together a long time. Where they came from, they were as free as that place let them be: hushed meetings with like-minded neighbours, careful not to ruffle feathers. Wee Barrow (22) may not be the first little they've shared, nor even the youngest, but he's been theirs for a few years now. Once they found him they knew there'd never be anyone else, and they came south and moved deep into the wood to live as they are, with no one watching. That's also why their home can be a safe house later: they know what it is to need one.

**Wee Barrow** has fully taken up his role. It's the life Red is only dipping her toes into, and he's committed to it. He's shy, but eloquent when it comes to why he lives this way. He's small and cared for, but not diminished by it. And it was never really a choice, except the choice between hiding it and living it. He's the one who can put into words what Red hasn't yet, which makes him a good voice for the conversation in step 4, and a mirror for her own path. (The draft lines below are a starting point.)

> "Folk think it makes me less. It doesnae. I'm still me, the whole of me. I just dinnae have to hold it all up by myself."
>
> "I didnae choose it, no really. The only choosing was whether I'd keep squashing it down or let it be. I tried the first one for years. This is better."
>
> "Bein' wee isnae the same as bein' nothing. They'd tell ye that themselves, an' they'd tan me if I ever said otherwise."

1. **2.1** Bo Peep's strays, a favour to a neighbour, draw Red deep into the wood. She finds the Barrows' house empty and unlocked, and helps herself: porridge, chairs, a bed.
2. **2.1** Found out. Mother Barrow corrects her, with Red's consent.
3. **2.1** Father Barrow then corrects Mother Barrow for leaving the house unsecured, and Wee Barrow for distracting her from securing it on their way out.
4. **2.1** An open conversation, all four of them. Red understands that accountability runs every way in their household. Mother and Father tell why they left where they came from. Wee Barrow, shy at first, explains why he lives as he does (see above), and Red hears her own half-formed thoughts put plainly.
5. **2.2** Return visits show more configurations: Mother Barrow correcting Wee Barrow for brattiness; on another visit, correcting Father Barrow for not upholding his role as head of household. Feeds Peter's line.
6. **3.2** Called back: they've seen Wolfe, and their home is a safe house.

**Bo Peep** (proposed) is Halloran's granddaughter, eighteen: the youngest of the cast, and grown. Her parents work the farm now that he can't, so she lives out there rather than in the village proper. Growing up largely apart from the village, she's felt younger for longer than the others did: she looks at Rose, and the rest of them, as grown-ups, and hasn't yet accepted that she's one herself. ## Bonus story: Peter and the Wolf

A possible DLC adapting Prokofiev's suite, either set earlier or reusing the characters with their history changed. It's played as Peter, with his grandfather, the bird, the duck, the cat, a wolf, and hunters who arrive too late and take the credit. Each character has a theme on its own instrument, as in the suite (Peter the strings, the bird the flute, the duck the oboe, the cat the clarinet, Grandad the bassoon, the wolf the horns, the hunters the woodwinds and drums), which could become motifs for our own cast too.

In it, Master Fowler is a corrector: Peter disobeys him and leaves the meadow gate open, and is taken in hand for it. His implement is his walking stick (proposed). In the main story he never needs to: Peter's steadiness means it's been years. His profile is in `js/correctors.js`, marked as the bonus story's, and Peter has a subject profile with his own safe word.
